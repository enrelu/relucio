Today, producing content is like leaving the tap running. Text, images, ideas… it makes no difference. It keeps pouring out. When that happens, the usual question—“who made it?”—starts to sound like an excuse. A person, an AI, a team: a way of avoiding what is actually in front of you.

I am not saying this to defend AI or take a swipe at anyone. Honestly, I do not particularly care. What matters to me is that **we are arguing about the wrong thing**.

For years, **authorship was a useful shortcut**. If a piece carried a respected name or came from a reputable source, it saved you some work. It was not foolproof, but it served as a cheap filter: “this is probably worth my attention.” The context has changed. **When production is extremely cheap and distribution is instant, the channel fills up.** Once that happens, the name on a piece matters less than the actual experience of reading it.

Authorship has not disappeared. Responsibility, track record, context, and reputation still matter. But they protect you less and less from one particular thing: **wasting time on pieces that are “fine” but leave you with nothing**. You have experienced this. You have read perfectly correct human-written texts that were… air. You have also seen AI-assisted pieces that, after good editing, had more substance than plenty of work with a name attached. Not because the machine is magic, but because **someone made decisions**.

Here is the shift: **the bottleneck is no longer creating. It is filtering.**

When information is abundant, scarcity moves elsewhere. **We are not short of content. We are short of attention.** And when attention is scarce, **the value lies not in producing more, but in cutting, organizing, and deciding**.

The worst part—or the most realistic—is that a few institutions used to do this work: editors, media organizations, universities, brands, people acting as gatekeepers. Now the gates are open and production costs almost nothing. The work lands on the reader. On you.

**If you do not filter, someone filters for you.** Usually with incentives that are not yours.

So what do we do? You need a practical test you can apply quickly, without turning it into an endless debate. I find it useful to think in two simple categories: things that have a shape but support nothing, and things that carry real weight.

The first kind looks right: a good structure, pleasant sentences, agreeable ideas, no risk, no trade-offs. You read it and it is not bad… but nothing happens. There is no tension. No clear decision. No idea that moves anything forward. In fact, there is often a telling sign: **you can summarize it in one sentence without losing anything**. That is because there was nothing dense inside it. It was filler with good diction.

You probably recognize it. On some social networks it is the norm, and I am definitely not looking at LinkedIn.

The second kind is the opposite: **content shaped by decisions**. You can tell when someone chose an angle and gave up the others. When they cut, tightened, and kept only what matters. When there is an internal truth—even a subjective one—and a clear intention. And yes, when it makes you look at something you would rather avoid. It does not have to be “art.” It can be useful, technical, practical. **But it carries weight.**

This is where we often get it wrong: confusing origin with quality. We build a convenient mental table—human equals good, machine equals bad—because it saves work. It is tempting. But it does not tell you what you really need to know.

Origin does not tell you whether there was editing. Whether someone cut the excess. Whether someone took a stand. Whether someone accepted a cost, even the cost of looking bad. **Quality does not come from the keyboard. It comes from judgment.**

And **judgment is not a recipe you can fully automate**, because it is not a checklist. It is a strange combination of taste, experience, courage, and the ability to say “no.” **Knowing what to remove.** Knowing where the nerve is. Knowing which sentence holds everything together and which is decoration. And, if you push me, knowing which part will unsettle the right reader.

**When production is cheap, that judgment becomes capital.** It separates content that merely takes up space from content that changes something inside you, even by a millimeter.

Here is a practical rule:

Whenever you consume something—text, video, audio, anything—ask yourself one simple question: **does this deserve space in my head?**

Not “did it entertain me?” Not “is it well made?” Not “who wrote it?” **Does it deserve that space?**

If the answer is no, out it goes. No negotiation. If the answer is yes, push a little further: what do I take away? What changes in how I think? What idea could I repeat tomorrow without embarrassment?

Here comes the part nobody particularly wants to hear, but it is the honest ending.

**The work is in your head.** Not in the model. Not in the author. Not in the algorithm.

**In you.**

**Discern. Edit. Discard.**

Because if you do not do it, someone will do it for you. And almost certainly, they will do it worse.
